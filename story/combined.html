The Big O

Pleasure is private and hard to put into words. We mostly assume it just happens during sex, and that it happens the same way for everyone.

Researchers have tested that assumption by asking thousands of people very personal questions and turning the answers into percentages. The percentages leave plenty out, but they're a good place to start.

So let's start with you.

*This part uses the six groups reported in one study. That isn't a complete list of identities, and the results describe the people who answered the survey, not what you will experience.

Why are the choices so limited?

Frederick et al. (2018) reported results for six groups: heterosexual, lesbian, and bisexual women, and heterosexual, gay, and bisexual men. The paper only reports men and women. It doesn't say whether the survey asked about gender or sex, doesn't mention trans or nonbinary people, and didn't ask about the partner's gender. Anyone outside these six groups isn't in the data.

Start with the two biggest groups. 95% of straight men said they usually or always orgasm when they're intimate with someone. For straight women, it's 65%.

Researchers call that 30-point difference the orgasm gap.

Frederick et al. (2018): 52,588 adults in relationships who answered an online survey on the NBC News website. The sample is big, but people chose to take part, so it doesn't represent everyone.

A common explanation is that women's bodies are harder to figure out, as if they come with complicated assembly instructions.

But, as you may have found, the same survey found something that doesn't fit. Among the lesbian women who took part, 86% said they usually or always orgasm, compared with 65% of the straight women.

Both groups are women, and their numbers are 21 points apart.

And the bisexual women in the study? 66%, almost exactly the same as the straight women, and 20 points below the lesbian women.

So in this survey, being a woman doesn't explain the gap on its own.

The survey didn't ask who people's partners were, so orientation is only a rough stand-in for who was in bed. The numbers describe the people who answered this survey, not lesbian or bisexual women in general.

Maybe the gap disappears with age. You'd expect people to know their bodies, their preferences and their partners better over time.

A 2009 national survey of 1,931 U.S. adults aged 18 to 59 asked about the last time they had sex with someone: did you orgasm?

This is a different question from the earlier one. It asks about a single event, the most recent, not how often people usually orgasm, so don't compare these numbers with the ones above.

Five age groups, from 18–24 up to 50–59, women and men side by side. Each drawing is as tall as its percentage.

In all five age groups, more men than women said yes.

In their late twenties, the gap is about 34 points: 91% of the men, 58% of the women. Among people in their fifties it's about 16: 87% vs. 71%.

Age seems to help a little, but in this survey it never closes the gap.

Herbenick et al. (2010), Table 3: the 2009 National Survey of Sexual Health and Behavior, a weighted national probability sample of adults who'd had partnered sex in the past year. These are different people at different ages, not the same people growing older, and the survey stops at 59. "Men" and "women" include some same-sex encounters.

Bring a hand (or a toy)

Hands came up on that menu too. A national survey of about 2,900 U.S. women asked whether they'd ever touched the clitoris during penetration, either themselves or a partner, with fingers or a toy, to orgasm more often or make sex feel better. Researchers call this pairing.

These are women who had ever done this, and plenty do more than one. The question asked about orgasm or pleasure, so a yes doesn't mean more orgasms. "Self-applied" means her own hand during penetration, not solo sex.

How long does it take?

There's also the old complaint that women "take forever." One study asked 2,304 women in the U.S. and Hungary how long it usually takes them.

The same women reported about 8 minutes on their own and about 14 with a partner. The men's times are shorter, but they come from a different study that only timed penetration to ejaculation.

These times are modeled. Only averages are published, so each person's time is drawn from a curve fitted around that average (a log-normal), and the spread is an assumption. Women's times: Rowland et al. (2018), self-reported, minute values via secondary coverage. The groups mix studies and measures. Sped up: 1 minute = 1 second.

So, about that gap.

None of these studies can tell you why the gap exists. They're snapshots, mostly of people who chose to answer.

They do show what didn't explain it. Getting older didn't close it, and being a woman didn't explain it: lesbian women in the same survey were at 86%. What went along with more orgasms was more of the menu, like oral sex, hands and kissing, the parts of sex that often get treated as extras.

One possibility is that the gap has more to do with what people count as sex than with their bodies. The data can't promise that asking for what you want, or bringing a hand along, will change anything, but both are easy to try.
