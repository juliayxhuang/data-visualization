Intro

Sex is often talked about as a shared experience, but the numbers suggest that pleasure isn't always shared equally.

In a study of more than 52,000 adults, 95% of heterosexual men reported usually or always orgasming during sexual intimacy, compared with just 65% of heterosexual women.

This 30-percentage-point difference is commonly referred to as the orgasm gap. But the more closely we look, the more complicated the picture becomes.

Source: Frederick et al. (2018).

Sexual orientation

If the orgasm gap were simply a difference between men and women, we might expect the numbers to look relatively similar within each gender.

But they don't.

While 65% of heterosexual women reported usually or always orgasming, the figure rose to 86% among lesbian women.

So what changes when we look beyond gender?

Source: Frederick et al. (2018).

Age

Maybe the gap is something that disappears with age. After all, we might expect people to become more familiar with their bodies, preferences, and partners over time.

But a 2024 study of nearly 25,000 adults found that the orgasm gap persisted across age groups, from young adulthood to later life.

Getting older, it seems, doesn't automatically close the gap.

Source: Gesselman et al. (2024).

Relationship context

But perhaps who you're with—and how well you know them—matters.

Research comparing hookups and established relationships found substantial differences in women's reported orgasm frequency. In one study, orgasms were reported in 11% of first hookups, compared with 67% of sexual encounters within relationships.

These numbers raise another question: how much of sexual pleasure depends on the circumstances surrounding it?

Source: Armstrong, England & Fogarty (2012).

Relationship structure

We often treat monogamy as the default model for a successful relationship. But does that mean it's necessarily the most satisfying?

A meta-analysis combining 35 studies and more than 24,000 participants found no statistically significant overall differences in sexual or relationship satisfaction between monogamous and consensually non-monogamous individuals.

Of course, satisfaction and orgasm frequency aren't the same thing. And perhaps that's exactly why the distinction matters.

Source: Anderson et al. (online 2025; issue 2026).
